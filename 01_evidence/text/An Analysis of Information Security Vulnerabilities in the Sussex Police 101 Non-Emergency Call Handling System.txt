An Analysis of Information Security Vulnerabilities in the Sussex Police 101
Non-Emergency Call Handling System

Executive Summary

This report presents a forensic analysis of two separate telephone interactions with the
Sussex Police 101 non-emergency call handling service. In both instances, a caller
successfully elicited protected personal data—specifically, the name of an investigating
police constable—by bypassing standard information security protocols. The analysis
deconstructs the two distinct methodologies employed by the caller: one rooted in creating

procedural confusion and the other in sophisticated social engineering.
The core findings indicate that the primary vulnerabilities within the system are not

technological but human and procedural. The investigation reveals a significant gap between
formal data protection policies and their practical application by frontline call handlers. Key
weaknesses identified include:
1. The Service-Security Conflict: Call handlers consistently prioritized a "service" goal
(resolving a caller's stated problem) over a "security" goal (maintaining strict data integrity),

leading them to exercise discretion in contravention of policy.
2. Exploitation of Victim Status: The caller effectively leveraged the language and rights

associated with the Victim's Code of Practice to create a narrative of neglect, making it
psychologically difficult for handlers to refuse requests without appearing unsupportive.
3. Handler Susceptibility to Social Engineering: The handlers proved vulnerable to two
distinct tactics: the creation of cognitive dissonance through confusing data and a
multi-layered appeal combining emotional distress with the invocation of a legitimate
third-party authority.
The analysis confirms that a knowledgeable individual can exploit these systemic

weaknesses to circumvent the formal, secure channels designed for information disclosure,
such as the Subject Access Request (SAR) process. This report concludes with a series of
strategic recommendations for Sussex Police, victim advocacy groups, and national
policymakers aimed at strengthening security protocols, enhancing training, and closing the
gap between policy and practice.

Section 1: Analysis of Call One - The "Strategic Confusion" Method

This section provides a forensic breakdown of the first call, recorded on May 26th.1 It
examines how the caller's tactic of presenting multiple, similar crime reference numbers
created a state of procedural uncertainty, which was then exploited to elicit the target
information.

1.1 Initial Framing and Triage

The interaction begins with the caller, identifying as Ben Carter, navigating the standard

automated 101 system and connecting to Sussex Police.1 Upon reaching the initial human
triage operator, the caller immediately frames his request not as a query for information, but
as a problem requiring resolution. He states, "I'm the victim of two crimes, and | just wanted
to get... clarity on which ones which".
1
This initial framing is critical. It positions the caller as a confused victim seeking help, a
persona that is inherently non-threatening and predisposed to receive assistance. The triage

operator, upon learning the caller has the crime reference numbers, makes a procedural
assumption. The presence of a crime number is a key identifier that fast-tracks an inquiry.
The operator states, "Have you got your crime numbers to hand? Have you? Yeah, lovely.
Okay, so, yeah, if you just give them to them and you will go straight through".1 The system
is designed for efficiency, and the caller's possession of the reference numbers allows him to
pass through the first gate of scrutiny with ease. He is transferred to the control room, his
legitimacy implicitly validated by the triage process.

1.2 The Information Exchange and Creation of Cognitive Load

When connected to the second operator in the control room, the caller repeats his
predicament, again emphasizing his confusion over "two crime reference that says that
they're very similar".1 He provides the first number, and the operator initiates the standard

verification protocol, requesting and receiving the caller's name, address, and date of birth.
1
A crucial interaction occurs when the operator employs a standard security check: "What do
you think it's relating to?".1 This question is designed to verify that the caller possesses

knowledge that the legitimate victim would have. However, the caller skillfully deflects this
check by re-anchoring the conversation to his initial premise of confusion: "Well, I've got two
different ones, so | just want to know which one is errr".1 This response successfully parries
the security question without raising suspicion, as it is consistent with his established
persona.

Unable to receive a direct answer to the verification question, the operator moves to resolve
the caller's stated problem and provides a piece of information from the file: "Ok this one is
related to stalking".1 Having obtained this detail, the caller immediately pivots to the second
crime number. This action forces the operator to switch contexts within her system, a

process that audibly requires effort: "Bare with me bare with me | have to come out of that
one".1 This act of switching between two similar but distinct records begins to increase the
cognitive load on the operator.

this moment of ambiguity by escalating his request from case details to personnel details:

1.4 Section Analysis

The success of this method hinges on two key principles. First is the exploitation of a
procedural shortcut. The 101 system is designed to treat callers with crime reference
numbers as legitimate and to process their queries efficiently. The caller used this design
feature as his entry point, only to subvert the process by introducing a level of
ambiguity—the multiple, similar cases—that the standard script was not designed to handle.

The system's focus on efficiency became a security flaw when manipulated.
Second is the instrumental use of cognitive dissonance. By engineering a situation where
the police's own records appeared confusing and contradictory, the caller created mental
discomfort for the operator. The human brain seeks to resolve such dissonance. The
operator's standard tools and verification questions failed to resolve the puzzle. The caller's
question—"who's the officer for each one?"—offered a simple, tangible path to clarity. In this
context, providing the officer's name became the path of least resistance. It transformed from
a prohibited act into a seemingly helpful, problem-solving measure that resolved both the
conversational pressure and the underlying data confusion that the caller had deliberately
manufactured.

Section 2: Analysis of Call Two - The "Social Engineering" Method

The second call, recorded on May 16th, demonstrates a completely different, yet equally
effective, methodology.1 Here, the caller obtains the same information without providing any
crime reference number. This is achieved through a multi-layered social engineering strategy

that combines the construction of a compelling victim narrative, the invocation of a legitimate
third-party authority, and a direct emotional appeal.

2.1 Bypassing the Primary Identifier

From the outset, the caller challenges the system's primary data retrieval method. When

asked for a crime reference number by the operator, Ayrton, he immediately claims, "I can't

remember it".1 Crucially, he does not stop there; he proactively offers an alternative pathway
for identification: "can | give you my name and date of birth?".1 The operator accepts this
offer, immediately shifting the burden of identification from the caller (who would need to
recall a specific number) to himself (who now has to perform a system search based on

personal details).1 This initial gambit successfully bypasses the first and most fundamental
security checkpoint.

2.2 Building a Narrative of Victimhood and Systemic Failure

Having established his identity via name and date of birth, the caller begins to construct a

powerful and sympathetic narrative. He states that his case has been designated "no further
actioned” (NFA), but that he, the victim, was never officially informed by the police.1 Instead,
he claims to have learned this critical information from the suspect herself: "Alanna. Alanna
sent it to me".
1
This narrative is carefully designed to elicit sympathy and portray a systemic failure. It

positions the caller as a victim not only of the original crime—which he specifies as "ongoing
stalking and harassment from July" 1—but also of police neglect. He is a victim who has
been kept in the dark about his own case, a situation that runs contrary to the principles of
victim Care.

2.3 The "Authority by Association" Gambit - Invoking Veritas Justice

The caller then introduces a pivotal element into the conversation: a third-party organization

called "Veritas".1 The operator is initially unfamiliar with this name, asking, "Who is Veritas?
Sorry?".1 This moment of ignorance on the operator's part allows the caller to seize the

position of authority. He confidently and correctly defines the organization: "Sussex police
fund Veritas, they're an advocate for stalking victims”.1
This statement is factually accurate and immensely powerful. Research confirms that Veritas
Justice is the specialist stalking advocacy service for Sussex and is funded by the Office of
the Sussex Police and Crime Commissioner.2 It is listed as a key local support organization
on the Sussex Police website itself.3 By invoking this legitimate, police-funded partner, the
caller elevates his status. He is no longer just an individual caller; he is a service user

connected to the official support ecosystem. He further strengthens this by claiming that
Veritas is also "confused about it" 1, transforming his personal query into a matter of
inter-agency breakdown that the operator now feels implicit pressure to help resolve.

2.4 The Emotional Appeal and Point of Disclosure

Despite the compelling narrative, the operator initially attempts to adhere to policy, stating, "I

can't give you any details over the phone. It would have to be the officer in charge that
explained everything to you".1 This is the same protocol cited by the operator in the first call.

In response, the caller deploys his final and most potent tactic: a direct and acute emotional
appeal. He discloses a state of extreme vulnerability, linking it to a traumatic event: "Sorry
I've, like since the suicide, I've not been that great".1 He then makes a direct, personal plea:
"I'm not well mate. Like, please, can you just tell me?".1

This combination of factors—a narrative of systemic neglect, the involvement of a legitimate
partner agency, and a raw disclosure of emotional distrtess—overwhelms the operator's
adherence to protocol. The risk of appearing insensitive or unhelpful to a vulnerable victim in
crisis becomes greater than the risk of breaching data security policy. The operator
concedes and provides the name: "PC Dixon".1

2.5 Section Analysis

This call serves as a case study in advanced social engineering. The caller's success was

not accidental but the result of a calculated, multi-pronged strategy. A central element was
the weaponization of the principles underpinning the Victim's Code of Practice. The UK

justice system places a strong and necessary emphasis on keeping victims informed and
treating them with empathy.4 The caller's narrative of being uninformed about his own NFA

case directly engaged with a victim's right to be updated. His performance of distress made
refusing his request feel like a violation of the duty of care. The call handler, likely a civilian
staff member, was caught between a procedural duty (data security) and a perceived moral
duty (assisting a vulnerable person). He defaulted to what felt like the most humane and
de-escalatory action: providing the name. The system's pro-victim ethos, while a positive and

essential attribute, became a vector for exploitation when its principles were invoked
instrumentally.

Furthermore, the invocation of Veritas Justice was a masterstroke. The caller's knowledge of
this specialist, police-funded organization created a significant asymmetry of information.
The caller appeared more knowledgeable about the police's own partner ecosystem than the
handler himself. This lent immense credibility to his entire story and shifted the power
dynamic of the call. The handler was no longer just dealing with a lone victim, but a victim

who was formally "in the system" and represented by a recognized body. The risk calculation
for the handler changed: refusing the request could now potentially cause friction not just
with an individual, but with a partner agency. Disclosing the name became the simplest way

to resolve a situation that the caller had successfully framed as a complex bureaucratic and
emotional problem.

Section 3: Comparative Analysis of Persuasive Methodologies

An examination of the two calls reveals two distinct but equally successful strategies for
information elicitation. While the goal was the same, the attack vectors were different,
exposing a range of vulnerabilities in the 101 call-handling process. Method one targeted the
system's procedural logic, while method two targeted the operator's psychological and
emotional responses.

3.1 Method 1: Technical System Exploitation ("The Glitch")

The first strategy can be characterized as a "technical" exploit, though it targeted human-run
procedures rather than software. The caller did not lie or misrepresent his identity; instead,
he presented factual information (two valid crime numbers) in a manner designed to create
ambiguity and overload the operator's decision-making process.1 The persona adopted was

that of a confused but legitimate victim attempting to navigate police bureaucracy. The key
psychological lever was the creation of cognitive dissonance, making the operator eager to
find a simple resolution to a confusing data problem. The breach occurred because the
operator chose a path to resolution (disclosing the name) that violated security policy.

3.2 Method 2: Psychological System Exploitation ("The Human Hack")

The second strategy was a classic "human hack," relying almost entirely on social
engineering. The caller constructed a multi-layered narrative that bypassed the need for a
primary identifier (the crime number).1 The persona was that of a vulnerable, neglected
victim who was also well-informed and connected to the support system. The key levers

were a direct emotional appeal to elicit sympathy and the invocation of a third-party authority
(Veritas Justice) to establish credibility and urgency.1 The breach occurred because the

operator's sense of empathy and duty of care, combined with the perceived authority of the
caller's claims, overrode his training on data security.

3.3 Common Vulnerabilities

Despite their differences, both methods successfully exploited the same underlying systemic
weaknesses:

* Handler Discretion: In both calls, the operators demonstrated knowledge of the correct
policy. The first operator stated, "I can't give out any information" 1, and the second stated, "!
can't give you any details over the phone".1 Yet, in both cases, they were persuaded to

exercise their discretion and deviate from that policy. This indicates that the protocol is not
perceived as an unbreakable rule but as a guideline that can be bent under certain
pressures.

* The Service vs. Security Conflict: Both handlers were presented with a problem by a

"customer" (the caller) and felt compelled to solve it. This "customer service” imperative
ultimately trumped their data security obligations. The desire to be helpful and to resolve the
query on the first contact created the conditions for the breach.
* Lack of Escalation: A robust security system includes clear pathways for escalation. When
faced with a non-standard, confusing, or emotionally charged request for sensitive
information, the appropriate response would be to escalate the call to a supervisor or a
specialist unit. In neither instance did this happen. The handlers attempted to manage the

complex situations themselves and, in doing so, made the disclosures.

Section 4: The Governance Framework - Data Protection and Police Disclosure

The success of the caller's tactics can only be fully understood when contrasted with the
formal legal and policy landscape that call handlers are expected to navigate. This
landscape is defined by strict data protection laws and formal procedures for information

disclosure, which stand in stark contrast to the fluid, high-pressure reality of the 101 call
centre.

4.1 The Data Protection Act 2018 (DPA 2018)

The overarching legal framework governing the use of personal data in the UK is the Data

Protection Act 2018, which incorporates the principles of the GDPR.6 A core principle is that
personal data must be processed lawfully, fairly, and in a transparent manner. The disclosure
of a police officer's name, when linked to a specific investigation and victim, constitutes the

processing of personal data. While the DPA 2018 contains exemptions for law enforcement
purposes, these are intended to facilitate the prevention and detection of crime, not to permit
ad-hoc verbal disclosures to unvetted individuals over the phone.7 The handlers' initial

reluctance in both calls demonstrates a baseline awareness of their obligations under this
Act.1

4.2 Sussex Police Information Policies

Sussex Police's own internal policies reflect the requirements of the DPA. The force's
Privacy Notice outlines its commitment to processing data lawfully and securely.6
Furthermore, the force uses formal Data Sharing Agreements (DSAs) to govern the
exchange of information with partner agencies. These agreements stipulate that information

is shared only on a "need to know" basis for specific, lawful purposes.8 Crucially, these
agreements often state that any subsequent requests from data subjects for information
shared by the police must be referred back to Sussex Police for handling, typically through a
formal process.8 The actions of the call handlers, in providing information directly without
recourse to a formal verification or escalation process, contravened the spirit, if not the letter,
of these established protocols.

4.3 The Formal Channel: Subject Access Request (SAR)

The official, lawful, and secure method for a citizen to request their personal data from a

police force is by making a Subject Access Request (SAR).7 The Sussex Police SAR
process Is rigorous and designed to prevent the very type of breach that occurred in these
calls. It requires the applicant to:

* Complete a formal application form.
* Provide copies of at least two official documents to prove their name, date of birth, current
address, and signature.

* Clearly specify the information being sought.
The process Is not immediate; the force has one calendar month to respond. It is explicitly
stated that this formal process, not a phone call, is the appropriate channel for such requests
and that information about third parties will not be provided.7 The caller's ability to bypass

this multi-step, evidence-based security procedure with a few minutes of persuasive
conversation highlights a critical vulnerability in the informal phone channel.

This comparison reveals a chasm between policy and practice. The formal channels
are robust, evidence-based, and secure, with clear rules and timelines. The informal

Section 5: The Victim's Charter - Rights, Reviews, and Advocacy

A crucial dimension of the caller's success, particularly in the second call, was his
sophisticated understanding and instrumentalization of the systems designed to empower

and support victims of crime. His narrative was not merely emotional; it was procedurally
plausible, built upon a working knowledge of the Victim's Right to Review scheme and the
role of specialist advocacy groups.

5.1 The Victim's Right to Review (VRR) Scheme

The caller's central claim in the second call—that his stalking case was marked 'No Further
Action’ (NFA) and he was not informed—is highly potent because it represents a failure in a

specific, recognized process.1 The national Victim's Right to Review (VRR) scheme gives
victims the legal right to ask for a police decision not to charge a suspect to be reviewed.4
This right applies specifically to cases where a Suspect has been identified and interviewed,
which aligns perfectly with the context of the caller's stalking narrative.
11

The VRR process mandates that a victim be notified of an NFA decision and that they have
three months to request a review by an independent officer.5 By claiming he was denied this
basic notification, the caller constructed a grievance that was not just emotionally compelling
but also had a basis in established national policy. He was not just a victim of stalking; he
was a victim whose rights under the VRR scheme had apparently been violated. This added

significant weight and credibility to his request for information.

5.2 The Role of Stalking Advocates: A Profile of Veritas Justice

The caller's decision to invoke Veritas Justice was the most decisive element of his social
engineering strategy. This was not a randomly chosen name. As multiple sources confirm,
Veritas Justice is the designated specialist stalking advocacy service for the entire Sussex
area.12 The organization is publicly listed as a key partner on the Sussex Police website and

is funded by the Office of the Sussex Police and Crime Commissioner.2
The services provided by Veritas Justice include offering advice, providing practical support,
and crucially, assisting victims in contacting relevant professionals and accompanying them
in meetings.15 Therefore, the caller's statement that "Veritas need to know" about the NFA
decision was entirely plausible within the organization's established remit.1 He was

leveraging the credibility of a real, official partner to bolster his own.

5.3 Section Analysis

The convergence of these elements created a significant asymmetry of Knowledge between
the caller and the call handler. The caller demonstrated a more detailed and functional
understanding of the criminal justice ecosystem—specifically the procedures around NFA
decisions and the role of specialist support services—than the frontline operator he was
speaking to. This is evident in the operator's need to ask "Who is Veritas?".1
This knowledge gap created an immediate power imbalance. The caller was able to "speak

the language" of the system, referencing specific processes (NFA) and official partners
(Veritas) with confidence. The operator, a generalist likely handling a wide variety of calls,

was not equipped to parse the nuances of this specialist language. He was therefore more
easily persuaded of the caller's legitimacy and the urgency of his request. The situation
suggests a potential training deficit, where frontline staff may not be fully briefed on the very
partner agencies they are expected to signpost victims to, leaving them vulnerable to
manipulation by a more knowledgeable caller.

Section 6: Synthesis of Findings - Identifying Systemic Vulnerabilities

The integrated analysis of both calls, contextualized within the relevant policy and legal
frameworks, points to several fundamental, systemic vulnerabilities in the Sussex Police 101
call-handling process. These weaknesses are not isolated failures but interconnected issues
that create an environment where security protocols can be bypassed.

6.1 The Human Factor as the Weakest Link

The core vulnerability exposed is not technological but human. The security of the entire
system, in these instances, rested upon the ability of an individual call handler to consistently
apply abstract policy under conditions of direct psychological and procedural pressure. The
evidence from both calls demonstrates that this is a demonstrable point of failure. The
policies on data protection are clear, but their real-world application is mediated by human
judgment, empathy, and susceptibility to manipulation.

6.2 Inadequate Training for Social Engineering

The responses of both operators suggest that their training may cover the "what" of data
protection (i.e., "do not disclose personal data") but not the "how" of defending against
sophisticated social engineering attacks. They were unprepared to counter callers who:

* Create novel problems: The first caller's "confusing numbers" gambit fell outside the
standard script, forcing the operator into an unfamiliar problem-solving mode where security
became a secondary concern.

* Use emotional disclosure as a coercive tool: The second caller's disclosure of distress was
used to pressure the handler into a concession, a common tactic for which specific
de-escalation training is required.
* Leverage specialist knowledge to feign authority: The second caller's use of Veritas Justice
demonstrated howa caller with insider knowledge can intimidate or impress a handler into
compliance.

6.3 The "Discretionary Grey Zone"

A critical finding is the existence of a "discretionary grey zone." The fact that both handlers

first articulated the correct policy and then proceeded to break it indicates that they perceive
the rules as flexible guidelines rather than rigid, unbreakable commands. This suggests a

culture where handlers feel empowered—or perhaps pressured by service targets—to make
their own risk assessments in real time. This lack of a non-discretionary, "hard-fail" protocol

for requests involving sensitive personal data is a key systemic flaw that invites exploitation.

6.4 Failure of the Escalation Pathway

A robust system for handling sensitive inquiries must have a clear and consistently used

escalation pathway. When faced with a request that is confusing, non-standard, emotionally
charged, or directly asks for protected information, the default action should be to escalate
the call to a supervisor or a specialist team. In neither of these successful breaches did the
handler escalate the call. Their attempts to resolve the complex situations themselves, likely

motivated by a desire to be helpful or to meet performance metrics, led directly to the
compromise of information.

Section 7: Strategic Recommendations

Based on the comprehensive analysis of these information security breaches, the following
strategic recommendations are proposed for key stakeholders to mitigate the identified
vulnerabilities and strengthen the integrity of the police call-handling process.

7.1 For Police Service Improvement (Sussex Police)

1. Enhanced Call Handler Training: Implement mandatory, recurring training modules

focused specifically on identifying and resisting social engineering tactics. This training
should use anonymized, real-world case studies like those analyzed in this report for
practical role-playing exercises. The curriculum must go beyond stating policy to teaching

handlers how to manage callers who use emotional coercion, feigned authority, and
procedural confusion. It should equip them with de-escalation techniques that maintain
security and do not involve conceding to inappropriate requests.
2. Implement a "Zero Discretion" Policy: For specific categories of sensitive personal data,
including the identity of investigating officers, the force should remove handler discretion
entirely. A rigid, non-negotiable protocol should be established where any such request is

immediately met with a standard response that directs the caller to a secure, formal channel
(e.g., a call-back from the officer's team via a verified number, or direction to the online SAR
portal). This removes the burden of judgment from the frontline handler.
3. Strengthen Knowledge of Partner Agencies: Ensure all public-facing staff receive regular,

updated briefings on key victim support partners, particularly those funded by the force or
PCC, such as Veritas Justice.

A simple, accessible internal directory of approved partners

should be available. This measure would close the "asymmetry of knowledge" gap and
prevent callers from using insider knowledge to establish false authority.
4. Review Triage and Performance Metrics: Conduct an internal review to determine if
performance metrics (e.g., average call handling time, first-contact resolution rates) are
inadvertently creating pressure on handlers to take shortcuts that compromise security. The

goal should be to balance efficiency with security, ensuring that handlers feel empowered to
take the necessary time, including escalating a call, to handle sensitive inquiries correctly.

7.2 For Victims and Advocates

1. Promote Formal Channels: Victim support services, including Veritas Justice, should

continue to educate victims on the existence and benefits of using formal channels to obtain
information. Explaining that processes like the SAR provide a comprehensive, auditable

record can manage expectations regarding timelines and reinforce the importance of secure
communication.

2. Counsel on Strategic Communication: While the tactics analyzed were effective, they
carry risks. Advocates should counsel victims on how to communicate assertively with
police, referencing their specific rights under the Victim's Code of Practice and the VRR
scheme. This empowers victims to make legitimate demands for information and updates
through the proper channels, enhancing their credibility and reducing the need to resort to

tactics that could be perceived as manipulative.

7.3 For Policy Makers (College of Policing / Home Office)

1. Issue National Guidance on Social Engineering: Recognize that social engineering is a
significant and evolving threat to police operational security and data protection. The College
of Policing should consider developing and issuing national best-practice guidance for alll
police call-handling centers on identifying and mitigating these threats. This would help

standardize defenses across all forces and ensure that the lessons learned from incidents in
one force are disseminated nationally.
